# Photovoltaic conversion

A photovoltaic cell turns light into electricity without any moving parts. Photons
striking a doped silicon wafer free electrons across a junction, and the resulting
potential difference drives a direct current through an external circuit. Commercial
silicon modules convert roughly a fifth of the incident energy; laboratory multi-junction
designs reach substantially higher figures at a cost no rooftop installation would bear.

Module output degrades slowly, on the order of half a percent per year, so a panel
installed today is expected to deliver most of its rated output three decades later.
Temperature matters more than most buyers expect: a cell loses efficiency as it heats, so
a cool bright day yields more than a hot one of identical illumination.

Balance-of-system costs — mounting, wiring, inverters, labour — now dominate the price of
an installation, because the modules themselves have fallen further and faster than
anything around them.

Inverters are the component most likely to fail first. A string inverter carries a
warranty measured in a decade where the modules carry three, so an installation costed
over its full life has to budget for at least one replacement. Microinverters move that
failure to the roof, which trades a cheaper unit against a more expensive visit.

Shading is not proportional. A string of series-connected cells passes the current of its
weakest member, so a single shaded cell can suppress the output of an entire string until
bypass diodes route around it. This is why a chimney's shadow costs far more generation
than its area suggests, and why layout matters more than nameplate capacity on a
constrained roof.

Recycling remains the unsolved end of the chain. The glass and aluminium are
straightforward; separating the encapsulant from the silicon economically is not, and
most decommissioned modules are still landfilled rather than recovered.
